# SeedLend customer discovery

_Last consolidated: 12 September 2026._

This document records the first in-person SeedLend interviews and founder observations. It is **early qualitative/quantitative discovery**, not statistically representative market research and not proof of product-market fit.

## First interview round — La Paz, 11 September 2026

- 16 university students interviewed in person.
- The sample came from a **private university** and included students from financial/business-related and health-related fields.
- 15 of the 16 were approximately 18–21 years old according to the interview sheet summary.
- Interviews were conducted in **four groups**, so peer influence must be considered when interpreting answers.
- Founder clarification of group composition:
  - Group 1: 2 men + 3 women.
  - Group 2: 3 women.
  - Group 3: 3 men + 2 women.
  - Group 4: 3 men.
  - Total from this clarification: 8 men + 8 women.
- This group composition supersedes an earlier assistant-generated summary that had incorrectly described the sample as 10 women / 6 men.

## Interview questions used

The interview guide included the following questions, with some answers first collected as open responses to avoid priming:

1. Age: 18–20 / 21–23 / 24–25 / 26+.
2. When you have money available after necessary expenses, what do you normally do with it? Spend it / bet it / buy crypto / invest it / other.
3. Have you ever bet money on football, sports, online casino or another platform? Never / once / sometimes / frequently.
4. If you have bet, how much do you approximately use per bet/session? <$5 / $5–10 / $11–25 / $26–50 / >$50.
5. Have you ever bought cryptocurrency mainly expecting to profit from a price increase? Yes / No; if yes, which ones?
6. Do you currently have any savings or investment? Open response first.
7. How much money do you think you need at minimum to make your first investment? Open response.
8. During school or university, were you taught practically how to save, invest, understand compound interest and evaluate risk? Yes / Some / Very little / Never.
9. When should a person start learning about investment? Before 15 / 15–17 / 18–20 / 21–25 / after 25 / don't know.
10. How much do you think a course that truly teaches professional investing costs? Open response.
11. If a free practical investment course of one or two weeks existed, would you finish it? Yes / Probably / Probably not / No; then ask what it would need to include.
12. If tomorrow you had an additional USD 10 not needed for basic expenses, what would you do with it? Open response first.
13. After the behavioral questions, explain SeedLend briefly: “Imagine you can access a first USD 100 investment today without having the full USD 100, pay it little by little in small installments, and the financed money can only be used for that investment.” Then ask: Would you be interested in using something like this? Why or why not?
14. What betting, crypto, exchange or similar app have you used because a friend recommended it or influenced you to use it?

A separate expected-interest/return question was also discussed but was not part of the exact list reproduced above.

## Quantitative signals from the first 16 interviews

The current working counts recorded from the interview spreadsheet/analysis are:

- **11/16** said they normally spend their available money after necessary expenses.
- **6/16** had bet money at least once.
- **3/16** had bought cryptocurrency mainly expecting price appreciation.
- **15/16** reported having some form of savings or investment.
- **10/16** believed they needed at least **USD 500** to begin investing.
- **6/16** believed they needed at least **USD 1,000**.
- **4/16** believed they needed **USD 10,000 or more**.
- **11/16** directly said they had not received practical financial education.
- When asked what they would do with an extra USD 10, **15/16** said they would save at least part of it.
- **16/16** said people should begin learning about investing before age 18 in the interview summary.

Important interpretation:

> The first sample does not suggest simply that “young people have no money.” It suggests that many have small amounts, some ability/intention to save or pay over time, but may lack a clear path and may substantially overestimate the capital required to start investing.

A particularly useful contrast is:

> **11/16 normally spend available money, but 15/16 would save at least part of a concrete extra USD 10.**

This is an early signal that intention to build capital may exist even when the prevailing habit is spending.

## Follow-up interest

The founder asked **eight specific interviewees** for their phone number so he could create a WhatsApp group and keep them informed about SeedLend's progress.

**All eight people who were asked provided their number.**

This must not be reported as “8 of 16 showed interest,” because the other eight were **not asked**. The defensible statement is:

> “We asked eight interviewees whether they wanted to stay updated on the project, and all eight gave us their number.”

This is an early follow-up signal, not evidence of conversion, willingness to borrow, willingness to pay or product-market fit.

## Qualitative observations from the groups

### Peer influence

Group context visibly influenced answers. The first or second participant's response sometimes affected later responses.

One female participant said, in substance:

> “When I'm with you, I always spend.”

This is useful qualitative evidence that financial behavior can be socially influenced. It supports investigating university cohorts, peer accountability and community-based acquisition, but does not prove that SeedLend groups will improve repayment.

### Betting, crypto and risk behavior

The founder observed a strong pattern in this sample:

- none of the women interviewed reported betting or buying crypto in the situations described by the founder;
- some women nevertheless recognized or watched betting apps through male friends' phones;
- women more often expressed information-seeking/caution, e.g. wanting to research first and being unsure whether information was reliable;
- men more often expressed a “you win and you lose” framing and were more familiar with betting than with investing;
- the all-male final group of three reportedly all bet and all had Binance;
- bettors described betting as frequent behavior;
- some men focused strongly on the potential return and size of the financed position.

One notable response was approximately:

> “If it's only $100 in one year it's very little, but if it's $10,000 and you only have to pay $30 each month, that's more attractive.”

Interpretation:

- expected upside is likely a major adoption driver for a return-seeking segment;
- cautious/information-seeking users may value security, explanation and lower perceived risk more strongly;
- gender appeared correlated with these behaviors in this first small convenience sample, but **gender is not proposed as a credit/underwriting criterion**;
- the more useful product segmentation to test is **cautious/information-seeking vs. return-seeking/risk-tolerant**;
- more samples are needed before making population-level claims.

The founder expects regional/context differences (for example Santa Cruz may show different female betting behavior), which is another reason not to generalize from the first sample.

## Financial-knowledge observations

Participants showed strong interest in learning how to make money, but practical financial-system knowledge appeared limited.

An example raised during discussion was that none of the interviewed students knew, before it was explained to them, how a pay-as-you-go pension system uses contributions from current workers to fund current retirees.

This is a founder observation from discussion, not a scored item in the formal questionnaire and should not be converted into a population statistic.

## Product hypotheses strengthened by the interviews

The first round strengthens—but does not prove—the following hypotheses:

1. Many young adults overestimate the minimum capital needed to start investing.
2. There is a gap between intention to save/build capital and actual recurring behavior.
3. Practical financial education is a real barrier.
4. Return expectations materially affect interest in an investment-credit product.
5. Peer groups influence financial behavior and may become a distribution/accountability mechanism.
6. University communities may be useful because students spend several years together and can repeatedly see one another's progress.
7. SeedLend should not rely on an “education is enough” message; the economic motivation to build wealth/investment capital must remain central.

## Product hypotheses created/refined after interviews

- Show users the percentage of the investment they have progressively acquired after each repayment (“You now own X%”). This is intended to motivate continued repayment by making asset accumulation visible.
- Run a free public/required introductory course each month, both to educate users and continually acquire new cohorts.
- Require theoretical + practical assessment before expanded investment autonomy.
- Allow qualified users to redistribute/diversify their position after demonstrating understanding.
- Explore scheduled monthly repayments, a short grace period and behavioral incentives for on-time repayment.
- Explore university cohorts, peer learning, social proof and potentially group/accountability mechanisms.
- Study group-microfinance precedents such as social collateral/joint-liability models, without assuming they transfer directly to SeedLend.

## Research discipline

For all future public claims:

- do not describe these 16 interviews as representative of LATAM;
- do not claim causal relationships from group behavior;
- do not claim gender-based risk differences as established facts;
- do not call phone-number collection a waitlist unless participants explicitly join a waitlist;
- preserve memorable quotes as approximate unless exact wording was recorded;
- distinguish founder observation, interview count, external statistic and product hypothesis;
- compare group interviews separately from anonymous/individual responses because peer influence may change answers.
